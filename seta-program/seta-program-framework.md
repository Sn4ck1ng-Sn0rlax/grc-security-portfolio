# Security Education, Training & Awareness (SETA) Program Framework

A reusable framework for designing and running an organization-wide SETA program. Built from practical experience designing and delivering staff security training that measurably reduced user-driven errors and improved compliance in a live operational environment.

## 1. Program Objectives

- Reduce human-driven security incidents (phishing, weak credentials, unsafe data handling)
- Build a baseline of security literacy across all staff, regardless of technical role
- Create a culture where reporting a suspected incident is fast, easy, and blame-free
- Support compliance obligations tied to data protection and organizational policy — many data protection frameworks, including Kenya's Data Protection Act (2019) and the EU's GDPR, explicitly require organizations to ensure staff handling personal data are aware of their obligations, making SETA a compliance requirement, not just a best practice

## 2. Audience Segmentation

Not all staff need the same training. This framework splits the audience into three tiers:

| Tier | Audience | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | All staff | Phishing recognition, password hygiene, physical security, incident reporting |
| Tier 2 | Staff handling sensitive data (finance, HR, client-facing roles) | Data classification, secure handling, third-party sharing rules |
| Tier 3 | IT/technical staff and administrators | Secure configuration, privileged access management, incident response basics |

## 3. Delivery Methods

- **Live onboarding session** (30–45 minutes) for all new hires within their first week
- **Quarterly refresher micro-sessions** (10–15 minutes), delivered in team meetings to keep security top-of-mind without disrupting productivity
- **Simulated phishing campaigns**, run quarterly, with immediate just-in-time coaching for anyone who clicks rather than punitive action
- **One-page reference guides** for common scenarios (e.g., "What to do if you suspect a phishing email," "How to handle a client's personal data")

## 4. Sample Training Curriculum (Tier 1 — All Staff)

1. Why security awareness matters — real-world consequences framed around the organization's own mission, not abstract fear
2. Recognizing phishing and social engineering — live examples, red flags, and the "pause before you click" habit
3. Password hygiene and multi-factor authentication — practical setup guidance, not just theory
4. Physical security basics — clean desk practice, visitor protocols, device locking
5. How and when to report a suspected incident — the exact steps, with reassurance that reporting early is always the right call

## 5. Metrics That Matter

Avoid vanity metrics like "percentage of staff who attended training." Track outcomes instead:

- **Phishing simulation click rate** over time (target: consistent quarter-over-quarter decline)
- **Time to report** a suspected incident (target: under 30 minutes from receipt)
- **Password-related helpdesk tickets** (target: measurable reduction as MFA and password managers adopt)
- **Post-training quiz scores**, used diagnostically to identify which topics need reinforcement, not to penalize staff

## 6. Governance and Ownership

- Program owner: IT/Security lead, accountable for content currency and delivery cadence
- Executive sponsor: a senior leader who opens the onboarding session personally, signaling that security is a leadership priority, not just an IT checkbox
- Review cycle: full curriculum review every 12 months, or immediately following any real incident that reveals a training gap

## 7. Common Pitfalls This Framework Avoids

- **Death by annual compliance video**: one long, ignored session per year does not change behavior. Short, frequent touchpoints do.
- **Punitive phishing simulations**: publicly shaming staff who click erodes trust and discourages honest incident reporting. Coaching beats punishment.
- **One-size-fits-all content**: a finance team member and a junior developer face different real risks and need different training depth.
